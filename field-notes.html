Field Notes

The episode series

Short lessons from the studio's social channels, written up in English. The original episodes are in Arabic on Facebook and Instagram.

01

Why global designs can't be cloned into Algerian homes

Episode 01Reel · Climate
A glass box that works in Rotterdam overheats in Algiers. The climate, not the trend, should set the rules.

Trends travel faster than climate. A plan drawn for a temperate city arrives in Algiers with the same glazing ratios, the same thin walls, and the same expectation of mechanical cooling. The result is a house that is uncomfortable for five months and expensive for twelve.

  • Glass area should follow orientation, not fashion.
  • Thin walls have no thermal lag; they heat up with the day.
  • The Kasbah solved this with mass and shade centuries ago.
02

The house breathes

Episode 02Reel · Airflow
Cross-ventilation is not a window on each side. It is a pressure difference you design on purpose.

Opening a window on each side of a room is not cross-ventilation. Air moves when there is a pressure difference. We create one by placing inlets on the cool, shaded side and outlets on the warm, sunlit side, and by sizing the inlet smaller than the outlet so the air speeds up as it enters.

  • Inlet low and shaded, outlet high and sunny.
  • Smaller inlet than outlet: the Venturi effect.
  • Keep the path between them free of doors that will be closed.
03

Concrete and brick are heat batteries

Episode 03Reel · Materials
Thermal mass absorbs heat by day and releases it at night. Used well it cools you; used badly it cooks you.

Concrete and brick store heat. That is neither good nor bad; it depends where the mass is and when it releases. A west wall in full afternoon sun will radiate into the bedroom at midnight. The same wall shaded, or moved inside the envelope, becomes a cool reservoir.

  • Mass on the inside, insulation and shade on the outside.
  • Night ventilation flushes stored heat out of the mass.
  • Roofs need the most attention: they see the sun all day.
04

Stairwells as chimneys

Episode 04Reel · Airflow
A vertical void pulls hot air up and out. The stack effect is free, silent, and needs no electricity.

Warm air rises. A stairwell that runs from the ground floor to a vent above the roof turns into a chimney. The stack effect works day and night, in still air, with no moving parts. It is the cheapest cooling system we know.

  • A vertical void with a high outlet.
  • Inlets at the lowest, coolest point of the house.
  • Close the outlet in winter: the effect does not stop by itself.
05

Light shelves and the circadian home

Episode 05Reel · Light
Daylight bounced deep into a room does more than save power. It sets your body clock.

A light shelf is a horizontal surface below a high window. It shades the lower glass and bounces daylight onto the ceiling, which carries it deep into the room without glare. The effect is a brighter, calmer room and a body that knows what time it is.

  • Light shelves on south and east faces.
  • Bedrooms: morning light, evening shade.
  • Views to sky and horizon placed on purpose.
06

The kitchen as engineered medicine

Episode 06Reel · Ergonomics
Golden zones between 70 and 150 cm, drawers instead of cupboards, task light at CRI 90 and above.

The kitchen is where the body works hardest at home. Storage between 70 and 150 cm is the golden zone; drawers beat cupboards because you see everything at once. Task light at 90 CRI or above shows food as it really is.

  • Golden zone: 70 to 150 cm.
  • Drawers, not deep cupboards.
  • Task lighting at CRI 90+, on the work surface, not behind you.
07

A green facade on a small budget

Episode 07Reel · Planting
Local succulents, a light soil layer, and a simple frame. No fantasy budget required.

A living facade does not need an irrigation computer. A steel or timber frame, a light soil layer, and succulents that already grow in the region will cool the wall surface by several degrees and the air passing it. Water use is small and maintenance is seasonal.

  • Local succulents, not imported species.
  • A light substrate keeps the load off the wall.
  • Green roofs follow the same logic with a thin soil layer.
